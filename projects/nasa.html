Why we changed the question

We started with a question about crater size. When we checked the data, we found diameter estimates and relative velocities, but lacked impact angles and composition. After discussing those gaps with our advisor, Dr. Antonio Tovar, we shifted our focus to how asteroid size and speed affect estimated energy.

My contribution

I helped clean and analyze the asteroid parameters in Python and Excel. We used pandas and NumPy to work with the diameter ranges and compare modeled energies. Our Jupyter notebook records the steps so another reader can follow the assumptions behind the figures.

What did our sample look like?

Our final presentation covered 1,771 asteroids with close approaches between July 2024 and July 2026. We reported diameters from 5 to 750 meters and relative speeds from 0.7 to 25 miles per second (slide 7). Our diameter chart shows why the full range only tells part of the story. Small asteroids appear far more often than large ones.

How did we interpret the 3%?

In our presentation, we used asteroid size and proximity to Earth to discuss potential hazard. We reported that about 3% of our sample was potentially hazardous (slides 16–18). The percentage describes how our team classified the sample. Estimating the likelihood of a collision would require a separate orbital analysis.

Why are there two sample counts?

The archived NASA JPL export contains 1,912 close-approach rows. An asteroid can appear in more than one encounter. Our final slides summarize 1,771 asteroids and do not document every step between those counts. I use the presentation sample for the findings shown here.

Sharing the results

We presented Cosmic Fallout to faculty, students, and NASA representatives. To make the difference in scale easier to see, we mapped three hypothetical scenarios using Chicago as a reference point. The circles illustrate how our model behaved under different assumptions. Chicago was a location we chose for the demonstration.
