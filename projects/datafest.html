The question

At the American Statistical Association’s DataFest at Loyola University Chicago, our four-person team had three days to analyze healthcare records from Stormont Vail Health. I wanted to understand what the total encounter count hid. A patient seen once and a patient seen ten times each contribute one person to the dataset, but very different numbers of visits.

My contribution

I helped clean and combine the encounter and patient files in pandas. We grouped encounters by patient ID, classified patients by how often they returned, and compared the mean visit count across birth-year bins. We also grouped records by census block group to explore a geographic view.

Who enters the visit count?

Our left join produced 5,463,776 encounter rows. We found 321,736 rows without a match in the patient file. The notebook’s frequency groups count nonmissing patient-table keys. An unmatched row adds zero to that patient’s linked visit count.

How did use vary by birth cohort?

The notebook counted encounters per patient, then averaged those totals within each available birth-year bin. The 1935 bin averaged 37.2 encounters per patient, compared with 8.6 in the 2000 bin. These are totals over the recorded period. Differences in observation time or group composition could affect the comparison.

What I took away

Before interpreting utilization, I need to know which encounters can be linked and who is in the denominator. These counts describe recorded use. Explaining why patients returned would take additional evidence about their care and time in the dataset.
